Istanbul · Fascinating Chaos

A travel diary by Lorella

The bridge and the city’s souls

If there is one place that defines Istanbul as I see it, it is not a single palace or mosque, but a meeting point: a pulsing connection that perfectly embodies the fascinating, chaotic essence I have always felt here.

Water divides; the bridge embraces the Golden Horn’s nocturnal soul.

It is Galata Köprüsü, the Galata Bridge, seen at night. This is what my gaze tries to capture: a scene alive with contrasts, the dark, restless waters of the Golden Horn crossed by a horizontal river of light. Here Istanbul’s many souls reveal themselves.

We begin in Stamboul, on the southern shore, in Eminönü. This is the imperial and religious soul: the land of sultans, Topkapı Palace, the domes of Hagia Sophia and Sultanahmet Camii, the Blue Mosque. Roman, Byzantine and Ottoman history lives here.

The roots of Constantinople

It is fascinating to see how Constantinople’s DNA remains legible in modern Istanbul. Today’s Fatih district largely corresponds to the city shown on this map: Byzantine traces are still there, often hidden in plain sight.

Our walk begins at the Land Walls, the Theodosian Walls, on the western side of the map. These are the legendary defences breached by Sultan Mehmed II, Fatih the Conqueror, in 1453. Huge cannons helped the Ottoman siege, including those made by a Hungarian founder named Orban.

According to the traditional account, Orban first offered his services to the Byzantines, who could not afford his demands. Mehmed instead financed his work. The enormous bombards battered the walls around the Gate of St Romanus, associated with today’s Topkapı area: this sector was central to the final Ottoman assault and the entry into the conquered city.

A little farther south, another story catches the eye. Altınkapı, the Byzantine Golden Gate, was the Eastern Roman Empire’s ceremonial entrance. After the conquest, Mehmed incorporated it into Yedikule, the Fortress of the Seven Towers. Ottoman towers embrace the magnificent marble arches of the Byzantine imperial gateway: two eras joined in one structure.

Instinctively, the eye recognises what remains of that legendary armour. The Theodosian Walls still stretch for kilometres, a serpent of stone and towers marking the edge of the old city within the modern metropolis. Walking beside them brings a thousand years of Byzantine history within reach, and the moment that world ended.

Inside the walls, many churches marked on the map survived through their conversion into mosques.

Our first encounter with these survivors is the Church of the Holy Saviour in Chora, now Kariye Mosque. Its exterior reveals a layered history: Byzantine brick and stone, domes and apses, and the minaret the Ottomans added after converting the church into a mosque.

Inside is one of its treasures. A sudden gleam draws the eye to a Byzantine mosaic of Christ Pantocrator, ruler of all, holding the Gospel. Chora’s golden tesserae catch the light, making the sacred images shimmer and offering worshippers a vision beyond the everyday world.

Continuing our walk through the map, we discover other hidden jewels.

Here is the Church of the Virgin Pammakaristos, now Fethiye Mosque. It housed the Greek Orthodox Patriarchate after the fall of the city. Its parekklesion, or side chapel, is a treasure chest of sacred art.

Inside, the eye wanders among golden mosaics and rests on the majestic Christ Pantocrator in the parekklesion, amid prophets and sacred figures: an image whose beauty and spirituality recall the mosaics of Chora.

Nearby stands the imposing Monastery Church of Christ Pantocrator, now Zeyrek Mosque, one of Istanbul’s largest surviving Byzantine religious complexes after Hagia Sophia. It is not a single church but three interconnected buildings, a giant of brick overlooking the hill.

Nearer the sea, the map marks the Church of Saints Sergius and Bacchus, now known as Little Hagia Sophia, Küçük Ayasofya Camii.

Its nickname invites comparison with Justinian’s great basilica. Built in the sixth century, it belongs to the same extraordinary age of architectural experimentation. At dusk, its layered history becomes clear: Byzantine brickwork, the Ottoman columned porch, the son cemaat yeri, and the minaret added when it became a mosque.

Inside, the eye climbs the marble columns to the intricate, segmented dome. The interior has changed over the centuries, yet its proportions still convey the ambition and architectural inventiveness of Justinian’s time.

The best-known traces await us in the heart of the old city.

Along the main street, our gaze meets the Column of Constantine, Çemberlitaş. Darkened by time and bound with iron hoops, it is the city’s symbolic navel, erected in AD 330 to celebrate the inauguration of Constantine’s New Rome.

Our walk reaches the Hippodrome, now the spacious Sultanahmet Square. Monuments that once adorned the centre of the racing circuit, the spina, still stand, defying the millennia.

In the half-light, the eye questions two witnesses in stone: the Obelisk of Theodosius, brought from Egypt in the fourth century, and the Walled Obelisk behind it. Remnants of Byzantine grandeur, they now watch over the square’s night-time life.

The Church of Holy Wisdom, Hagia Sophia, is today Ayasofya. Almost in awe, our gaze rises to Constantinople’s emblematic dome against the sky. This is the map’s Ch. of S. Sophia, a monument that has endured for centuries and still evokes the ancient city.

Another perspective appears, framed by the dark geometry of a grille. Beyond the garden and palms, Hagia Sophia’s dome and minarets shine against the blue sky: a thousand-year-old monument glimpsed through a threshold of everyday Istanbul.

But Constantinople’s heart did not beat only above ground. Nearby lies the Sunken Palace: the Basilica Cistern.

Here the gaze descends into darkness and water, losing itself in a forest of 336 marble columns. At the far end it meets a myth: two heads of Medusa, one inverted and the other on its side, reused as column bases. Ancient imagery became part of the Christian city’s foundations; the exact reason for their positioning remains uncertain.

Walking through Fatih today means walking through the remains of ancient Constantinople. Its urban framework, walls and surviving monuments bear witness to centuries of history.

Before leaving this world of stone and centuries, our gaze takes one last breath: a flight over the Theodosian Walls. Suspended between past and present, we say farewell to Constantinople from above before returning to Istanbul today.

The Theodosian Walls from above: Constantinople’s last breath.

At last, filled with history, the gaze widens. From the ancient map we turn to a panorama of Istanbul today. The Süleymaniye fills the foreground; beyond its domes lie the Golden Horn, Galata Bridge and the Bosphorus. European and Asian shores, ferries, yachts and distant high-rises reveal a vast city that has grown around its ancient heart. One photograph cannot contain it all, but it hints at how far it stretches.

Eminönü and Galata Bridge

After our walk through the past, we return to the present. To me, no place embodies a beating heart quite like Eminönü Meydanı. It is not a square in the orderly, Italian Renaissance sense. It is a vital junction, a glorious anthill where the city meets the water.

Eminönü, the beating heart: a river of faces beneath imperial domes.

The quays are the real protagonists. From here the vapur, the ferries, depart tirelessly, stitching Europe to Asia, travelling up the Golden Horn or along the Bosphorus. The air overwhelms the senses: the metallic echo of trams, simit sellers’ calls, gulls’ cries and the unmistakable smell of balık ekmek, fish sandwiches.

Beyoğlu from Eminönü’s quays: an invitation to dialogue across the Golden Horn.

Overlooking this bustle, almost guarding the bridge’s entrance, stands Yeni Camii, the New Mosque. Its name is a gentle deception: it has been “new” for almost four centuries.

Yeni Camii: stone at prayer beneath an ink-dark sky.

Yeni Camii, Ottoman elegance: a needle embroidering Istanbul’s night.

Yeni Camii: a starry sky of lamps in a garden of tiles.

Yet the bridge itself is this chapter’s true protagonist. Galata Bridge is much more than asphalt: it is a stage on two levels, a floating neighbourhood.

Lines cast into history: Galata’s fishermen, a ritual between sky and water.

The lower level offers a different world: food and rest, with a long succession of restaurants.

Dining above the water, where the shores and their souls look towards each other.

Galata and Pera

Crossing the bridge means leaving one world and entering another. We reach Karaköy and begin to climb. The hill of Beyoğlu, formerly Pera, welcomes us with a different energy.

Climbing towards another soul: echoes of Europe on Galata’s cobblestones.

As the photograph shows, lanes narrow, cobbled streets climb and the architecture changes: neoclassical, fin-de-siècle, European façades. This is the city’s other, Levantine soul.

The golden hill: Europe’s gaze at sunset.

And above it all stands Galata Kulesi, the Galata Tower. More than a point on the horizon, it is a physical presence, a pivot around which neighbourhood life revolves.

Beneath the Genoese sentinel: Beyoğlu’s young heart.

The gaze of the Christea Turris: seven centuries overlooking the Golden Horn.

The square beneath it teems with a young, cosmopolitan crowd, unlike the more traditional atmosphere of Eminönü. This is the Genoese tower, the Christea Turris, Europe’s gaze watching the old city across the water for nearly seven centuries.

At the top comes the reward. The gaze takes in Istanbul’s many souls: Galata’s red roofs below, Galata Bridge across the Golden Horn, the historic peninsula of Fatih. We recognise Eminönü and Yeni Camii, the Spice Bazaar entrance and, majestic on its hill, the mosque of Süleyman the Magnificent. Fascinating Chaos, seen from above.

Beyoğlu and Pera’s European soul has its own glittering drawing room: the Pera Palace Hotel.

The iconic Pera Palace: more than a hotel, the Orient Express travellers’ luxurious retreat and Istanbul’s cosmopolitan drawing room.

The Pera Palace emblem woven into the entrance carpet: the red of luxury and 1892, the year it all began.

The Pera Palace’s Kubbeli Saloon: an immersion in Belle Époque elegance.

The historic Pera Palace lift.

Its historic electric lift bears witness to that age: a jewel of wood and wrought iron that carried kings, spies, writers and diplomats between sumptuous floors, recalling the excitement of the early electric era.

Atatürk’s room at the Pera Palace.

Room 101 was Mustafa Kemal Atatürk’s familiar lodging during his stays in the city. Today its memories evoke the founder of the Republic and the years in which the nation’s future was taking shape.

Another of Istanbul’s fascinating contradictions: memories of the Republic live in the heart of its most cosmopolitan, international quarter.

A modern hull cuts through the Bosphorus’s historic waters. Beyond it, the city reveals its inseparable weave of wealth, ancient history and teeming life.

The Fascinating Chaos I speak of is more than disorder. It is the vibrant tension between imperial and Levantine, Ottoman and Genoese, traditional and modern worlds, facing one another across the water. Water separates these souls, while the pulsing bridge draws them into an eternal embrace: one complex city with many souls.

Final glimpses. Sacred and secular, ancient and modern, joy and protest mingle on walls and water. This is Istanbul: a city whose different souls coexist in an impossible, wonderful balance. Everyday colours climb walls laden with history and politics; age-old lights reflected on the Bosphorus meet the ferries’ ceaseless smoke.

Everything is contradiction; everything is harmony. That, in the end, is Fascinating Chaos.

But our journey through the straits has only begun. Leaving Istanbul’s vibrant chaos behind, our route leads towards the other sister, watching over the Atlantic: Tangier, the Sentinel.
